Synthetic Cancer Cell Image Generation

Oleksandr Kardash

This project investigates whether GAN-generated synthetic histopathology images can improve the performance of a deep-learning classifier for breast cancer tissue. A conditional StyleGAN2-ADA model was trained on the BreaKHis dataset to produce realistic synthetic images, which were then combined with real images to train a benign-versus-malignant classifier. The project's central finding is that the apparent classifier improvements from synthetic augmentation depend on whether the test patients were also seen by the GAN during its own training, raising questions about how synthetic data is evaluated in this kind of pipeline.

Project Snapshot

Generating imagery to support medical understanding

Cancer diagnosis depends heavily on pathologists examining tissue images under a microscope. Deep-learning classifiers can support this process, but they require large amounts of training data — and medical datasets are often limited by collection cost, expert labelling effort, and patient privacy regulations. One commonly proposed solution is to train a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) to produce additional synthetic images that supplement the real training set. This project tests whether that approach genuinely helps a tumour classifier.

A conditional StyleGAN2-ADA model was trained on the BreaKHis dataset of breast cancer histology, separately for each of its four magnification levels (40x, 100x, 200x, 400x). The trained generators were evaluated quantitatively, with results indicating high image quality. A separate check confirmed that the generated images were not direct copies of the training set. The synthetic images at 40x magnification were then used to train a ResNet-18 binary classifier on the benign-versus-malignant task.

Four classifier experiments of increasing rigour were carried out, culminating in a controlled A/B evaluation that isolated a single variable: whether the test patients had been seen by the GAN during GAN training, or not. Both experiments used matched training set sizes, validation set composition, and class proportions, with the only difference being the identity of the test patients.

The result was a clear asymmetry between the two groups. On test patients the GAN had previously seen, adding synthetic images improved classifier accuracy by about ten percentage points. On patients the GAN had never seen, the improvement was less than one point. The apparent benefit of synthetic augmentation was therefore tied to overlap between the GAN's training set and the classifier's test set, rather than reflecting genuine generalisation to new patients. Identifying the exact cause of this asymmetry — candidates include GAN overfitting, distribution bias, and amplification by the dataset's crop structure — would require further experiments beyond the scope of this project.

Focus

Investigating whether GAN-generated synthetic histopathology images can genuinely improve a downstream tumour classifier, with particular attention to whether observed gains generalise to patients the GAN has never seen.

Audience

Researchers and practitioners working on synthetic data augmentation in medical imaging, especially those building or evaluating GAN-based pipelines for downstream classification tasks.

Demo

A side-by-side comparison of real and GAN-generated histopathology images, followed by a walkthrough of the controlled GAN-seen vs GAN-unseen evaluation and its asymmetric results, showing how apparent gains from synthetic augmentation can fail to carry over to unseen patients.
